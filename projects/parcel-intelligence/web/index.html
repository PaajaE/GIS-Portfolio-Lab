    Problem

    Early site screening usually reduces a parcel to one number: its area minus mapped constraints, or a parcel-average slope. Both hide the internal structure that decides whether anything can be built — a parcel can have plenty of unconstrained area that is split into slivers by a wetland buffer, a steep bank and the setback from its own lot lines. The question here is: given a parcel and stated planning assumptions, how much of it remains potentially feasible, which constraints dominate, and how robust is that conclusion?

    Why it matters

    Housing committees, land trusts and site-selection analysts need to decide which parcels deserve a closer look — survey, soils tests, legal review. A screen that overstates usable land wastes that effort; one that hides how much the answer depends on a threshold invites false confidence. This screen reports the contiguous patch, not the sum of fragments, and shows the result under three assumption sets rather than one.

    Study area and analytical unit

    Town of Amherst, Massachusetts: … assessor parcels (FY2026, condominium stacks merged), hilly in the south (Holyoke Range) and east, with broad floodplains and wetlands along the Fort River and Mill River. The parcel is the reporting unit, but feasibility is computed on a … m grid in NAD83 / Massachusetts Mainland (EPSG:26986) so that the shape of the feasible area inside each parcel is kept.

    Method

    1. Hard constraints are rasterized per scenario at cell centres: flood zone, wetland/water/stream buffer, protected land, priority habitat (conservative), slope at or above the threshold, a uniform setback from parcel lines, and existing buildings and road surface.
    2. Morphological opening removes slivers: a cell stays only if a … m square of feasible cells of the same parcel contains it.
    3. Connected components (4-connected, never across parcel lines) give the largest contiguous patch and the number of patches of at least … m².
    4. Baselines: a naive parcel-level screen (parcel-mean slope and a single point test) and the net unconstrained area (parcel minus constraints, no contiguity).
    5. Leave-one-out: each site constraint is dropped in turn to find the one whose removal enlarges the largest patch most (the binding constraint).

    Rust / algorithm

    The opening and component labelling must respect parcel boundaries: a feasible cell in one parcel must never help its neighbour pass the opening or join its patch. The reference implementation loops over parcels with scipy.ndimage; the Rust kernel does the same in one raster pass (separable run-length erosion and dilation conditioned on parcel id, then union-find). Both produce identical integers on the full town and on 72 randomized fixtures.

      Limitations — what this screen cannot tell you

      • Not entitlement. Zoning dimensional rules, permitted uses, septic suitability (Title 5), soils, ledge, access easements and wetland delineation in the field are not modelled. Wetland buffers here approximate regulated areas; they are not determinations.
      • Uniform setback. The setback from lot lines is one value per scenario, not the district-specific front/side/rear yards.
      • Flood data vintage. FEMA NFHL as republished by MassGIS (FEMA data as of 2023-07-02). Later map revisions are not included; FEMA's own service was not reachable from the build machine.
      • Parcel geometry. Assessor maps are not surveys; a 2 m grid misplaces boundaries by up to a cell. Area error vs exact overlay is reported in Validation.
      • Terrain. Bare-earth lidar is post-construction and interpolated under buildings; graded house pads read as flat.
      • Road surface uses an assumed half-width by road class, not measured pavement.
      • Distances are straight-line from the patch to a road centreline — not access or travel time. Frontage is a geometric proxy, not legal frontage.
      • Tax-exempt land (municipal, state, institutional) is shown but not screened.
